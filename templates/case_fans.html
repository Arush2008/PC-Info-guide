Case Fans

Moving Air, Managing Heat.

Case fans are the lungs of your PC. They create the airflow that removes heat generated by every component. The type, size, placement, and quality of fans determine your system's cooling performance, noise levels, and overall thermal efficiency.

What It Does

Case fans move air through the computer case, carrying heat away from components. Intake fans pull cool air into the case, while exhaust fans push warm air out. A well-designed fan setup can significantly reduce temperatures compared to poor airflow, allowing components like the CPU and GPU to maintain higher performance.

How It Works

Fan blades are designed like airfoils - their shape and angle determine whether they prioritize airflow (CFM) or static pressure (mmH2O). A fan's motor spins using electromagnetic forces, controlled by either PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) or DC signals from the motherboard. PWM allows the motherboard to automatically adjust fan speed based on temperature.

Why It Matters for Gaming

Every major PC component produces heat, including the CPU, GPU, VRMs, RAM, and SSDs. Without proper airflow, heat builds up inside the case, causing higher temperatures and possible thermal throttling. Many cases include only basic fans, so adding quality fans with correct airflow direction can significantly improve cooling and system stability.

Types & Variants

120mm Fan

Radiators, compact builds, universal setups

The most common fan size. Fits almost all PC cases and can be used for case airflow, radiator cooling, or CPU cooler fans.

Pros:

  • Universal compatibility
  • Huge variety of options
  • Easy to replace
  • Works well on radiators

Cons:

  • Moves less air than 140mm fans at similar noise levels
  • Requires higher RPM for the same airflow

140mm Fan

Large cases, quiet airflow-focused builds

A larger fan size that can move more air at lower RPM compared to 120mm fans, making it popular for case intake and exhaust.

Pros:

  • Higher airflow at lower RPM
  • Quieter for similar airflow levels
  • Excellent for larger cases

Cons:

  • Not supported in all case mounting locations
  • Usually slightly more expensive

High Static Pressure Fan

AIO radiators, CPU heatsinks, restricted airflow areas

Designed with specialized blades that push air through resistance, making them ideal for radiators and dense heatsinks.

Pros:

  • Best performance through restrictive surfaces
  • Ideal for AIO radiators and CPU coolers
  • Maintains airflow against resistance

Cons:

  • Usually louder at maximum speed
  • Less efficient in open airflow situations

High Airflow Fan

Case intake and exhaust positions

Designed to move large amounts of air through open spaces with minimal resistance.

Pros:

  • Excellent for case ventilation
  • Moves large amounts of air
  • Usually quieter in open-air positions

Cons:

  • Poorer performance through radiators and heatsinks
  • Not suitable for restrictive airflow areas

PWM Controlled Fan

Modern gaming PCs - recommended standard

Uses a 4-pin connection to receive temperature-based speed control signals from the motherboard. Fans slow down during light workloads and increase speed under heavy loads.

Pros:

  • Automatic temperature-based control
  • Quiet during idle use
  • Better cooling control under load

Cons:

  • Requires a PWM-compatible motherboard header

Key Specifications

Airflow (CFM / m3/h)

Critical

Measures how much air the fan can move. Higher airflow is useful for unrestricted case positions such as front intake or rear exhaust. Around 50+ CFM is considered good for many 120mm case fans.

Static Pressure (mmH2O)

Critical

Measures how strongly a fan can push air against resistance. Higher static pressure fans are better for radiators, filters, and heatsinks. Around 2 mmH2O or higher is useful for restrictive applications.

Noise Level (dBA)

Important

Measures how loud the fan is. Quality fans often operate around 20-30 dBA, while cheaper fans can become much louder at high speeds.

Fan Speed (RPM)

Important

Higher RPM usually increases airflow but also increases noise. Around 1200-1800 RPM is a common balance for gaming PCs, while specialized fans can exceed 3000 RPM.

Bearing Type

Important

Fluid Dynamic Bearings (FDB) and high-quality bearings can last 100,000+ hours. Lower-quality sleeve bearings generally have shorter lifespans, especially in warm environments.

PWM vs DC Control

Important

PWM (4-pin) provides more precise speed control compared to DC (3-pin). Modern gaming systems generally use PWM fans for better noise and temperature management.

Real-World Gaming Impact

Case Temperature Comparison

A properly balanced setup with multiple intake and exhaust fans can significantly reduce GPU and CPU temperatures compared to a case with limited airflow.

Lower temperatures and better sustained performance

AIO Radiator Performance

Using high static pressure fans on an AIO radiator improves cooling because they can push air through the radiator fins more effectively.

Better CPU cooling performance

Positive vs Negative Pressure

Positive pressure (more intake airflow than exhaust) helps reduce dust entering through unfiltered gaps. Negative pressure can remove warm air quickly but may pull in more dust.

Cleaner system with controlled airflow

Silent Gaming at Idle

Quality fans can run at very low speeds during light workloads, creating a nearly silent PC experience while increasing speed only when needed.

Quiet everyday operation

Common Mistakes

  • Pointing all fans in the same direction - a proper airflow path needs both intake and exhaust.
  • Using high static pressure fans everywhere - they are best for radiators and heatsinks, not open case airflow.
  • Underestimating the importance of airflow - many budget cases include only one or two basic fans.
  • Ignoring dust filters on intake fans - filters reduce dust buildup and cleaning frequency.
  • Buying RGB fans without checking compatibility - ARGB (5V 3-pin) and RGB (12V 4-pin) connectors are different and not interchangeable.
